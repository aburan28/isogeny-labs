Kuperberg's algorithm and CSIDH

CSIDH was not broken. It was repriced, repeatedly, by analyses that disagree about what a quantum computer costs to run — and the disagreement is a more instructive object than either answer, because it is about cost models rather than about mathematics.

01The structure CSIDH chose

CSIDH, introduced by Castryck, Lange, Martindale, Panny and Renes, works with supersingular elliptic curves defined over Fp rather than over Fp2. Restricting to the prime field makes the endomorphism ring commutative — an order O in an imaginary quadratic field — and its ideal class group then acts on the set of such curves, freely and transitively:

cl(O) × Ellp(O) → Ellp(O),  ([a], E) ↦ [a] · E A free, transitive group action — a torsor, not a group

That action commutes, so key exchange is the obvious thing: Alice publishes [a]·E0, Bob publishes [b]·E0, and both compute [a][b]·E0. Nothing else is transmitted — and that absence is why CSIDH survived the attacks that ended SIDH. The prize is real: very small public keys, and genuine non-interactive key exchange, which no lattice-based KEM provides.

The price is the same commutativity. A group action whose group is abelian is precisely the setting of the abelian hidden shift problem, and that problem has a subexponential quantum algorithm.

02The mechanism

Recovering Alice's secret from [a]·E0 means inverting the group action. Define two functions on the group, one built from E0 and one from [a]·E0; they are equal up to a shift by the unknown [a]. Finding that shift is the abelian hidden shift problem, and Kuperberg gave a quantum sieve for it running in

2O(√(log N)) quantum queries and time, for a group of order N Subexponential — between Grover's √N and Shor's polynomial time

The sieve prepares many phase-encoded states over the group and combines them pairwise, each combination concentrating the unknown shift into fewer, more useful bits, until the shift can be read out. Regev gave a variant using polynomial quantum space at somewhat greater time; Kuperberg's second algorithm improved the tradeoff again. The important structural point is what the algorithm is not: it is not Shor. Commutativity is enough for a subexponential quantum attack and not enough for a polynomial-time one, which is exactly the narrow ledge CSIDH stands on.

Classically the picture is unremarkable. Inverting the action is a search over the class group, done by meet-in-the-middle or a random-walk collision search in Õ(√|cl(O)|), which for CSIDH parameters is about p1/4. Classical security therefore scales the way generic attacks always do.

03The dispute

The exponent is agreed. What is contested is the constant, the query count, and above all what a quantum query costs — and at subexponential asymptotics those decide everything at real parameter sizes.

  • The original proposal offered CSIDH-512 as a NIST level 1 parameter set, on an analysis of Kuperberg-style attacks available at the time.
  • Peikert gave a concrete analysis of the collimation sieve applied to CSIDH and concluded that CSIDH-512 falls well short of that claim — that the quantum work is far smaller than the proposers had estimated.
  • Bonnetain and Schrottenloher reached similarly reduced security estimates by a different route.
  • Bernstein, Lange, Martindale and Panny costed the quantum circuits for evaluating the group action in detail, arguing that the per-query cost of a real implementation is high enough to change the conclusion substantially.
  • Chávez-Saab, Chi-Domínguez, Jaques and Rodríguez-Henríquez took the conservative branch and showed that much larger parameters — CSIDH-4096 and beyond — remain implementable, if slower.

The disagreement is not about the mathematics. It is about the cost model: whether quantum random access memory is free or expensive, whether an attack is allowed unbounded circuit depth or must fit within a realistic time limit, and how much error-correction overhead a long-running quantum computation carries. Different defensible answers to those questions produce security estimates that differ by tens of bits.

What to ask

A quantum security claim is a claim about a cost model, and it is worth no more than the model it names. When someone quotes a post-quantum security level, the first question is which model produced it — depth-limited or not, QRAM free or costed. Two papers can be simultaneously correct and 40 bits apart.

The practical consequence today is a consensus that CSIDH-512 is not a level 1 parameter set, and disagreement about exactly how much larger is enough. CSIDH is not a NIST candidate and is not deployed at scale; it remains an active research object, valued for the non-interactive key exchange that nothing else in the post-quantum portfolio offers.

04The boundary

Kuperberg's algorithm needs the group to be abelian. That single hypothesis partitions the isogeny landscape cleanly:

  • CSIDH and CSI-FiSh use a commutative class-group action, so the attack applies and sets their quantum parameters.
  • SIDH and SIKE used the non-commutative supersingular graph over Fp2, so Kuperberg never applied to them — they fell to something else entirely, the torsion-point attacks.
  • SQIsign is likewise non-commutative, resting on the endomorphism-ring problem via the Deuring correspondence. Kuperberg does not reach it.
  • The general supersingular isogeny path problem has no known subexponential quantum attack; claw-finding approaches give about p1/3 quantum against √p classical, with severe memory costs that argue against the quantum advantage being real.

Read the two isogeny pages in this library together and the trade is explicit. SIDH published extra data to make its non-commutative walk usable, and the extra data is what killed it. CSIDH needs no extra data because commutativity does that work — and commutativity is what exposes it to Kuperberg. Each scheme paid for its key exchange in a different currency, and the community found out what each price was, in one case abruptly and in the other by a decade of argument that is still running.
