Cryptanalysis reference

Notes on the attacks that public-key parameters are sized against. Each page says what the attack needs, what it costs, what the largest computation on record used, and when the attack stops applying. Every page cites the original papers, and those are the thing to read if you need precision.

How the pages are organised

Most published attacks don't break deployed systems. They improve an exponent, or need a structural coincidence that sensible parameters avoid, or assume an oracle nobody has. So the question each page tries to answer is what the attack would cost against a particular set of parameters, and what would have to change for that cost to become affordable.

Each page covers, in order:

  • The problem instance: what the attacker is given and what counts as success.
  • The mechanism: the idea that makes the attack cheaper than brute force. It is usually a collision, a relation, or a map to an easier object.
  • The cost: the asymptotic bound, and what the largest published computation actually used.
  • The boundary: the hypothesis the attack depends on, and so the parameter choice that makes it irrelevant.

A caveat that applies throughout. an asymptotic bound is not a security level. O(√n) group operations says nothing about memory, communication, or the constant factor, and those decide whether a computation is affordable. Where a record computation exists, we give its measured cost alongside the bound.

Generic group algorithms

These work in any cyclic group where you can compute the group operation and test equality. Shoup's lower bound says no generic algorithm can use fewer than about √p operations, where p is the largest prime dividing the group order, so these algorithms set the baseline every discrete-log parameter is measured against.

  • Pollard's rho

    The square-root generic attack that every elliptic-curve key size is calibrated against.

    Θ(√n) group operations

  • Pohlig–Hellman

    A discrete logarithm is only as hard as the largest prime factor of the group order — which makes group-order validation a security control, not a formality.

    O(Σ ei(log n + √pi))

  • Baby-step giant-step and kangaroos

    Deterministic √n time-memory tradeoffs, and Pollard's lambda method for logarithms known to lie in a short interval.

Index calculus and descent

Index calculus collects relations over a factor base and solves a linear system. It is the reason discrete logarithms in finite fields take subexponential time. Whether anything like it works on elliptic curves has been an open question since Semaev's 2004 paper.

  • Summation polynomials

    Semaev's construction turns point decomposition into polynomial system solving — and delivers a genuine speedup over extension fields while leaving prime-field curves untouched.

    Õ(q2−2/n) over Fqn, n fixed

  • Weil descent (GHS)

    Transport an elliptic curve over F2m to a higher-genus curve over a subfield, where index calculus already works.

    Subexponential on vulnerable binary curves

  • The number field sieve

    The L(1/3) algorithm behind every factoring and prime-field discrete-log record — and the reason RSA and finite-field Diffie–Hellman need 3072-bit parameters to match a 256-bit curve.

    LN[1/3, 1.923] for general N

  • Gröbner-basis solving and first fall degree

    How F4/F5 behaves on the polynomial systems that index calculus generates, and why the measured degree of regularity decides feasibility.

Lattice attacks

When a secret is partly known, from a few leaked bits, a biased nonce, or a small root of a modular equation, lattice reduction can often recover the rest. Many breaks of deployed ECDSA and RSA implementations come from this family rather than from any weakness in the underlying problem.

  • Hidden number problem

    A few bits of nonce bias per signature, collected over a few hundred signatures, recovers the private key outright — regardless of curve size.

    Seconds, given enough biased signatures

  • Coppersmith's method

    Finding small roots of modular polynomials, and the partial-key-exposure and small-exponent attacks on RSA that follow from it.

Isogeny attacks

Isogeny problems are the newest of these assumptions. SIKE was broken outright in 2022, after it had reached the fourth round of NIST's standardisation process, which is a good reason to read the attacks on its successors closely.

  • SIDH torsion attacks

    Kani's criterion turned the auxiliary torsion images published by SIDH into a polynomial-time key recovery — breaking SIKE during NIST standardisation.

    Polynomial time; SIKEp434 in about an hour

  • Kuperberg and CSIDH

    Commutativity buys CSIDH its small keys and non-interactive key exchange, and costs it a subexponential quantum attack — whose concrete cost is still disputed.

    Classical p1/4; quantum 2O(√log N)

  • Meet-in-the-middle and the supersingular path problem

    Delfs–Galbraith, claw finding, and what the √p barrier means for SQIsign parameters.
