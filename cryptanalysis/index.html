Read an attack the way
a parameter-setter does.

Most published attacks do not break anything. They move an exponent, or they require a structural coincidence that real parameters avoid, or they assume an oracle nobody can build. The useful question is never "is this broken?" but "what does this attack cost against my parameters, and what would have to change for it to matter?"

So each page here is organised around four things, in this order:

  • The problem instance. Exactly what the attacker is given, and what counts as success.
  • The mechanism. The one idea that makes the attack cheaper than brute force — usually a collision, a relation, or a structure-preserving map to an easier object.
  • The cost. An asymptotic bound, plus whatever the largest honest computation on record actually consumed.
  • The boundary. The hypothesis that makes the attack work, and therefore the parameter choice that makes it irrelevant.
The standing caveat

An asymptotic complexity is not a security level. A bound of O(√n) group operations says nothing about memory, communication, or the constant in front — and it is those, not the exponent, that decide whether a computation is affordable. Where a record computation exists, its measured resource use is worth more than the bound.

01 / GENERIC GROUP ALGORITHMS

Attacks that use nothing about the group.

These work against any cyclic group in which you can multiply and test equality. They set the ceiling every concrete discrete-log parameter is measured against, and Shoup's lower bound says no generic algorithm can do better than √p.

  • Pollard's rho

    The square-root generic attack that every elliptic-curve key size is calibrated against.

    Θ(√n) group operations

  • Pohlig–Hellman

    A discrete logarithm is only as hard as the largest prime factor of the group order — which makes group-order validation a security control, not a formality.

    O(Σ ei(log n + √pi))

  • Baby-step giant-step and kangaroos

    Deterministic √n time-memory tradeoffs, and Pollard's lambda method for logarithms known to lie in a short interval.

02 / INDEX CALCULUS AND DESCENT

Attacks that exploit the arithmetic underneath.

Index calculus collects multiplicative relations over a factor base and solves a linear system. It is why finite-field discrete logs are subexponential and RSA moduli must be large — and why the question of whether it reaches elliptic curves has occupied two decades of research.

  • Summation polynomials

    Semaev's construction turns point decomposition into polynomial system solving — and delivers a genuine speedup over extension fields while leaving prime-field curves untouched.

    Õ(q2−2/n) over Fqn, n fixed

  • Weil descent (GHS)

    Transport an elliptic curve over F2m to a higher-genus curve over a subfield, where index calculus already works.

    Subexponential on vulnerable binary curves

  • The number field sieve

    The subexponential L(1/3) algorithm behind factoring and prime-field discrete-log records, and what its precomputation phase means for reused parameters.

    Not written yet

  • Gröbner-basis solving and first fall degree

    How F4/F5 behaves on the polynomial systems that index calculus generates, and why the measured degree of regularity decides feasibility.

    Not written yet

03 / LATTICE ATTACKS

Attacks that turn partial information into a short vector.

When a secret is almost known — a few leaked bits, a slightly biased nonce, a small root of a modular equation — lattice reduction usually finishes the job. These are the attacks that break deployed systems whose underlying mathematics was never in question.

  • Hidden number problem

    A few bits of nonce bias per signature, collected over a few hundred signatures, recovers the private key outright — regardless of curve size.

    Seconds, given enough biased signatures

  • Coppersmith's method

    Finding small roots of modular polynomials, and the partial-key-exposure and small-exponent attacks on RSA that follow from it.

04 / ISOGENY ATTACKS

Attacks on the newest assumptions.

Isogeny problems are the youngest hardness assumptions in deployment-adjacent cryptography, and the only ones to have had a headline scheme broken outright after standardisation review had begun. That history is the argument for reading them carefully rather than the argument for avoiding them.

  • SIDH torsion attacks

    Kani's criterion turned the auxiliary torsion images published by SIDH into a polynomial-time key recovery — breaking SIKE during NIST standardisation.

    Polynomial time; SIKEp434 in about an hour

  • Kuperberg's algorithm and CSIDH

    Subexponential quantum attacks on the class-group action, and the parameter dispute they caused.

    Not written yet

  • Meet-in-the-middle and the supersingular path problem

    Delfs–Galbraith, claw finding, and what the √p barrier means for SQIsign parameters.
