Schemes

What each widely used scheme rests on, what it costs, and where it stands against classical and quantum attacks. ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SQIsign have full pages; the rest are summarised here, with links to the attacks that set their parameters.

At a glance

"Broken by Shor's algorithm" means broken by a large fault-tolerant quantum computer, which does not exist yet. It is the reason for the move away from RSA and elliptic curves, and the reason key exchange moves first: traffic recorded today can be decrypted later, while a signature only has to resist forgery for as long as it is relied on.

Key establishment

How two parties agree on a shared secret over a public channel: Diffie–Hellman key exchange, or a key-encapsulation mechanism (KEM), in which one side encrypts a fresh key to the other's public key.

  • ECDH (X25519, P-256)

    Diffie–Hellman on an elliptic curve. It is the standard key exchange in TLS 1.3, SSH and Signal, usually on Curve25519 as X25519.

    Rests on
    The elliptic-curve Diffie–Hellman problem, which as far as anyone knows is as hard as the ECDLP
    Standards
    RFC 7748 (X25519 and X448); NIST SP 800-56A with the curves of SP 800-186
    Sizes
    X25519: 32-byte public keys and shared secret. P-256: 33-byte compressed or 65-byte uncompressed public keys
    Best known attack
    Pollard's rho: about 2126 to 2128 group operations for 256-bit curves
    Quantum
    Shor's algorithm; Roetteler et al. estimate about 2,330 logical qubits for P-256

    See also: Full page

  • Finite-field Diffie–Hellman

    Diffie and Hellman's original 1976 key exchange, in the multiplicative group of a prime field. Largely replaced by ECDH, which needs far smaller parameters for the same strength.

    Rests on
    The Diffie–Hellman problem in Fp×, attacked through the discrete logarithm
    Standards
    RFC 7919 named groups for TLS; NIST SP 800-56A
    Sizes
    ffdhe3072: 384-byte public values
    Best known attack
    The number field sieve, largely a precomputation on the prime that is then reused for every key; this is what the Logjam attack exploited. The record is a 795-bit prime field (2019)
    Quantum
    Shor's algorithm

    See also: The number field sieve

  • ML-KEM

    The module-lattice KEM standardised in 2024, formerly CRYSTALS-Kyber. It is what replaces ECDH, at first alongside it as a hybrid.

    Rests on
    Module learning with errors (MLWE)
    Standards
    FIPS 203 (August 2024)
    Sizes
    ML-KEM-768: 1,184-byte encapsulation key, 1,088-byte ciphertext
    Best known attack
    Lattice reduction
    Quantum
    No known attack beyond small speedups to lattice sieving

    See also: Full page

  • HQC

    A code-based KEM that NIST selected in March 2025 as a backup to ML-KEM, chosen because it rests on different mathematics.

    Rests on
    Decoding random quasi-cyclic codes
    Standards
    Selected in NIST IR 8545 (March 2025). NIST expects to publish a draft standard and to finalise it in 2027
    Sizes
    HQC-128: 2,249-byte public key, 4,497-byte ciphertext
    Best known attack
    Information-set decoding
    Quantum
    No known efficient attack

  • Classic McEliece

    McEliece's 1978 code-based system, the oldest public-key design still considered secure: very large public keys and very small ciphertexts.

    Rests on
    Decoding a random-looking binary Goppa code
    Standards
    Under ISO standardisation. NIST's 2025 report (IR 8545) dropped it from NIST's current process, citing limited interest and the risk of an incompatible parallel standard, and said NIST may later base a standard on ISO's
    Sizes
    mceliece348864: 261,120-byte public key, 96-byte ciphertext
    Best known attack
    Information-set decoding, which decades of work have improved only modestly
    Quantum
    No known efficient attack

  • CSIDH

    An isogeny-based key exchange with small keys and a property no lattice scheme offers: non-interactive key exchange, like Diffie–Hellman.

    Rests on
    Inverting a commutative class-group action on supersingular curves over Fp
    Standards
    None; a research scheme
    Sizes
    CSIDH-512: 64-byte public keys
    Best known attack
    Classically about p1/4; quantumly, Kuperberg's subexponential algorithm, whose concrete cost is disputed
    Quantum
    Subexponential; CSIDH-512 is widely considered too small

    See also: Kuperberg and CSIDH

Signatures

How a message is bound to a key, so that anyone with the public key can check it. Post-quantum signatures cost more bytes than the schemes they replace, by very different amounts:

  • RSA

    The first widely deployed public-key system (1977), used for both signatures and encryption, and still common for signatures in web certificates.

    Rests on
    The RSA problem, taking e-th roots modulo N = pq, which is no harder than factoring N
    Standards
    PKCS #1 v2.2 (RFC 8017); FIPS 186-5 for signatures; SP 800-56B for key establishment
    Sizes
    RSA-2048: 256-byte modulus and signature; RSA-3072: 384 bytes
    Best known attack
    The number field sieve; the largest public factorisation is RSA-250, 829 bits, in 2020
    Quantum
    Shor's algorithm

    See also: Full page

  • ECDSA

    DSA on an elliptic curve, used in TLS certificates, hardware tokens, and the transactions of Bitcoin and Ethereum. Each signature uses a fresh secret nonce, and a nonce that leaks even a few bits loses the key.

    Rests on
    The ECDLP, and a uniformly random secret nonce for every signature
    Standards
    FIPS 186-5; SEC 1; ANSI X9.62
    Sizes
    P-256: 64-byte signature (r, s); 33-byte compressed public key
    Best known attack
    Pollard's rho on the curve, about 2128. In practice, keys are lost to biased or leaked nonces through the hidden number problem
    Quantum
    Shor's algorithm

    See also: Full page

  • EdDSA (Ed25519, Ed448)

    Schnorr-style signatures on Edwards curves, with the nonce derived deterministically from the key and message, which removes the most common way ECDSA keys are lost.

    Rests on
    The ECDLP on Curve25519 or Curve448
    Standards
    RFC 8032; added to FIPS 186-5 in 2023
    Sizes
    Ed25519: 32-byte public key, 64-byte signature
    Best known attack
    Pollard's rho, about 2126 for Ed25519. Deterministic signing is exposed to fault attacks instead
    Quantum
    Shor's algorithm

    See also: Full page

  • Schnorr signatures (BIP-340)

    Schnorr signatures on secp256k1, introduced to Bitcoin with the Taproot upgrade.

    Rests on
    The ECDLP on secp256k1
    Standards
    Bitcoin Improvement Proposal 340
    Sizes
    32-byte public key (x-coordinate only), 64-byte signature
    Best known attack
    Pollard's rho, about 2127 using the curve's extra automorphisms
    Quantum
    Shor's algorithm

    See also: The ECDLP

  • BLS signatures

    Pairing-based signatures that aggregate: many signatures, even on different messages, combine into one short signature. Ethereum's consensus layer depends on this.

    Rests on
    The computational Diffie–Hellman problem in the groups of a pairing, in practice on the curve BLS12-381
    Standards
    An IRTF CFRG draft (draft-irtf-cfrg-bls-signature)
    Sizes
    In Ethereum's choice, 48-byte public keys and 96-byte signatures
    Best known attack
    Rho on the curve groups, and the number field sieve in the pairing's target field, which has embedding degree 12
    Quantum
    Shor's algorithm

    See also: The ECDLP

  • ML-DSA

    The module-lattice signature standardised in 2024, formerly CRYSTALS-Dilithium: NIST's main replacement for RSA and ECDSA signatures.

    Rests on
    Module learning with errors for the key; module short integer solutions for forgery
    Standards
    FIPS 204 (August 2024)
    Sizes
    ML-DSA-65: 1,952-byte public key, 3,309-byte signature
    Best known attack
    Lattice reduction
    Quantum
    No known attack beyond small speedups to lattice sieving

    See also: Full page

  • FN-DSA (Falcon)

    Hash-and-sign signatures over NTRU lattices: the smallest public key and signature of NIST's lattice schemes, with a harder signing algorithm.

    Rests on
    Finding short solutions to linear equations over NTRU lattices (NTRU-SIS)
    Standards
    FIPS 206, in preparation; NIST describes the process as under way
    Sizes
    Falcon-512: 897-byte public key, 666-byte signature
    Best known attack
    Lattice reduction. Signing samples a discrete Gaussian with floating-point arithmetic, which makes safe constant-time implementations harder than for ML-DSA
    Quantum
    No known efficient attack

  • SLH-DSA (SPHINCS+)

    Signatures built from a hash function and nothing else. It is the most conservative assumption available, paid for in signature size and signing time.

    Rests on
    Security properties of the hash function, SHA-2 or SHAKE
    Standards
    FIPS 205 (August 2024)
    Sizes
    SLH-DSA-128s: 32-byte public key, 7,856-byte signature. The 128f variant signs faster with 17,088-byte signatures
    Best known attack
    Generic attacks on the hash function
    Quantum
    Only generic speedups, which the parameters account for

    See also: Full page

  • LMS and XMSS

    Stateful hash-based signatures: small and fast, but each one-time key must never be used twice, so the signer has to keep reliable state.

    Rests on
    Security properties of the hash function
    Standards
    RFC 8554 (LMS) and RFC 8391 (XMSS); NIST SP 800-208, for uses such as firmware signing where state can be managed
    Sizes
    Depend on the tree height and Winternitz parameter
    Best known attack
    Generic attacks on the hash, or, in practice, a signer that reuses state
    Quantum
    Only generic speedups

  • SQIsign

    Isogeny-based signatures with by far the smallest public key and signature of any post-quantum candidate, and slow, intricate signing.

    Rests on
    Computing the endomorphism ring of a supersingular elliptic curve
    Standards
    A round-3 candidate in NIST's additional-signatures process (NIST IR 8610, May 2026)
    Sizes
    Version 3.0, level I: 83-byte public key, 200-byte signature
    Best known attack
    Wesolowski's 2026 algorithm, in time and memory p1/3+o(1); version 3.0 raised its parameters in response
    Quantum
    No known efficient attack

    See also: Full page

Symmetric ciphers and hashes

Schemes with no public key. They carry the bulk of the data once a key is agreed, and quantum computers threaten them far less: Grover's algorithm at most square-roots the cost of a key search, and parallelises badly.

  • AES

    The block cipher behind most bulk encryption, usually in GCM mode.

    Rests on
    Twenty-five years of cryptanalysis; there is no reduction to a number-theoretic problem
    Standards
    FIPS 197
    Sizes
    128-bit block; 128-, 192- or 256-bit key
    Best known attack
    Biclique cryptanalysis recovers an AES-128 key in about 2126.1 operations, four times faster than brute force. In practice, table-based software leaks through cache timing; use AES instructions or bitsliced code
    Quantum
    NIST uses key search on AES-128 as the benchmark for its lowest post-quantum security category

  • ChaCha20-Poly1305

    A stream cipher and a one-time authenticator, designed to be fast and constant-time in software without special instructions.

    Rests on
    Cryptanalysis of the ChaCha permutation, and the security of Poly1305 as a one-time authenticator
    Standards
    RFC 8439
    Sizes
    256-bit key, 96-bit nonce, 128-bit tag
    Best known attack
    Published attacks reach 7 of ChaCha's 20 rounds
    Quantum
    Grover's algorithm on key search

  • SHA-2

    The SHA-256 and SHA-512 family, used almost everywhere a hash is needed.

    Rests on
    Cryptanalysis of the compression function
    Standards
    FIPS 180-4
    Sizes
    224- to 512-bit output
    Best known attack
    None on the full functions; collisions exist only for reduced-round versions. The Merkle–Damgård structure allows length extension, which is why keyed hashing uses HMAC
    Quantum
    Generic speedups only

  • SHA-3 and SHAKE

    Keccak, a sponge construction chosen by public competition in 2012. Its extendable-output forms SHAKE128 and SHAKE256 run inside ML-KEM, ML-DSA and SLH-DSA.

    Rests on
    Cryptanalysis of the Keccak-f permutation
    Standards
    FIPS 202
    Sizes
    224- to 512-bit output, or any length for SHAKE
    Best known attack
    Only reduced-round versions
    Quantum
    Generic speedups only

Broken or retired

Schemes that fell, and what finally ended them. Most of these failures were visible years before they became practical.

  • SIKE

    Supersingular isogeny key encapsulation, a fourth-round candidate in NIST's process, broken in 2022 in about an hour on a laptop.

    Rests on
    An isogeny problem made easier by the torsion-point images the protocol publishes
    What ended it
    The torsion-point attacks of Castryck and Decru, Maino and Martindale, and Robert, all in 2022
    Status
    Withdrawn; the attack is polynomial-time, so larger parameters do not help

    See also: SIDH torsion attacks

  • Rainbow

    A multivariate signature scheme and finalist in NIST's third round, broken in 2022.

    Rests on
    Solving structured systems of multivariate quadratic equations
    What ended it
    Beullens' 2022 key-recovery attack, which broke the level-I parameters in about a weekend on a laptop
    Status
    Not selected by NIST

  • DSA

    The original Digital Signature Algorithm (1991), over a prime field.

    Rests on
    The discrete logarithm in a subgroup of Fp×, and a random nonce per signature
    What ended it
    Not an attack: FIPS 186-5 (2023) no longer approves DSA for generating signatures, only for verifying old ones
    Status
    Retired in favour of ECDSA, EdDSA and ML-DSA

    See also: Hidden number problem

  • SHA-1

    The 160-bit predecessor of SHA-2.

    What ended it
    The first public collision in 2017 (SHAttered), then chosen-prefix collisions in 2020, which make forged certificates practical
    Status
    NIST plans to retire it from all uses by 31 December 2030

  • MD5

    A 128-bit hash function from 1992.

    What ended it
    Collisions found by Wang and collaborators in 2004, and chosen-prefix collisions from 2007; the Flame malware used one to forge a Microsoft code-signing certificate in 2012
    Status
    Broken for any use that needs collision resistance

  • DES and Triple DES

    The 1977 block cipher standard and its tripled extension.

    What ended it
    DES's 56-bit key was searched exhaustively in public in 1998. Triple DES's 64-bit block allows birthday attacks on long sessions, demonstrated as Sweet32 in 2016
    Status
    NIST disallowed Triple DES for encryption after 2023

  • RC4

    A stream cipher once used in both TLS and WEP.

    What ended it
    Biases in its keystream, which make plaintext recovery from many encryptions practical
    Status
    Prohibited in TLS since 2015 (RFC 7465)
